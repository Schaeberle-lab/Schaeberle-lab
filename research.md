# Research

Our research programme combines **microbiology, molecular biology, natural product chemistry, and bioinformatics** to discover, characterise, and engineer bioactive compounds from bacteria and other microorganisms.

---

## 1. Discovery of new natural products

We systematically explore under-investigated microbial habitats — marine sponges, insects, amphibian skin microbiomes, termite nests, and soil from remote regions — searching for bacteria with the potential to produce novel bioactive compounds. Our screening pipeline integrates:

- **Activity-guided fractionation** against clinically relevant Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens
- **Genome mining** using bioinformatic tools to predict biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in sequenced genomes
- **Metabolomics and molecular networking** (LC-HRMS, GNPS) to dereplicate known compounds and prioritise new scaffolds
- **High-throughput microfluidics and FACS** for accelerated isolation of rare producer strains

Key habitats we exploit include the Bacteroidetes phylum (a largely untapped source), marine-derived myxobacteria, and sponge-associated bacteria from Indonesia, Papua, and the Indo-Pacific.

---

## 2. Darobactin and BAM complex inhibitors

One of our most exciting ongoing programmes centres on **darobactin**, a ribosomally synthesised and post-translationally modified peptide (RiPP) that selectively kills Gram-negative pathogens by targeting the essential outer membrane protein assembly factor **BamA**.

Our contributions include:

- Discovery and characterisation of new darobactin analogs through genome and metabolome mining
- Engineering of darobactin derivatives via mutasynthesis and amber stop codon suppression for introduction of non-canonical amino acids
- Elucidation of the radical SAM oxygenase DarE responsible for ether crosslinking in darobactin biosynthesis
- Structural studies of the BAM complex inhibition mechanism in collaboration with the Radford and Ranson groups (Leeds) and the Collet group (Brussels)

Darobactin was first reported in *Nature* in 2019 and represents one of the most promising antibiotic leads against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens discovered in recent years.

---

## 3. Biosynthesis and synthetic biology

Understanding the enzymatic logic of natural product biosynthesis allows us to reprogram it. We use this knowledge to:

- Characterise individual biosynthetic enzymes *in vitro* and *in vivo*
- Develop heterologous expression systems in *E. coli* and other chassis organisms
- Engineer biosynthetic gene clusters to produce new-to-nature analogs
- Establish genome-guided production protocols for rare compounds

Recent highlights include characterisation of the darobactin halogenase DarH, the radical SAM enzyme DarE, and the PKS-NRPS hybrid polyoxyperuin from a marine *Streptomycete*.

---

## 4. Bacteroidetes as a natural product source

The phylum Bacteroidetes has been largely overlooked as a source of specialised metabolites. Our systematic investigation — published in a 2022 *Natural Product Reports* review — has revealed that many strains harbour diverse BGCs producing novel alkaloids, lipopeptides, and glycerophospholipids with antimicrobial and other biological activities.

We have established *Pedobacter* strains as a genetically tractable model system within this phylum, enabling direct functional studies of biosynthetic pathways.

---

## 5. Antiparasitic and antifungal natural products

Beyond antibacterials, we investigate natural products active against:

- **Parasitic infections**: natural cysteine protease inhibitors (falcitidin analogs) active against *Plasmodium*, identified through activity-guided screening
- **Fungal pathogens**: sesquiterpenes and other scaffolds from marine sponges and fungi
- **Biofilm-forming bacteria**: evaluation of darobactin B and colistin combinations in static and dynamic biofilm models

---

## Funding and collaborations

Our research is funded by the **German Research Foundation (DFG)**, **DZIF** (German Centre for Infection Research), **BMBF**, and the **Fraunhofer Society**. We maintain active collaborations with groups at the University of Leeds, UCLouvain, MIT, Northeastern University (Boston), Max Planck Institute for Plant Breeding Research, and partner universities in Indonesia, Peru, and Tunisia.
